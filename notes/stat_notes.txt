The word "batch"what are biased and unbiased estimators and what are differnt types of estimatorsAn estimator is a rule or formula used to guess the value of an unknown population parameter (like the true average height of everyone in a country) based on a sample of data.Biased vs. Unbiased EstimatorsThe core difference between a biased and an unbiased estimator lies in whether the estimator, on average, hits the exact target parameter over many repeated trials.Unbiased Estimator: An estimator is unbiased if its expected value (the average of all possible sample estimates) is exactly equal to the true population parameter. Even if individual samples over- or under-estimate the true value, the errors cancel out in the long run.Example: The sample mean (X̄) is an unbiased estimator of the population mean (μ).Biased Estimator: An estimator is biased if its expected value systematically under- or over-estimates the true population parameter. The distance between the expected value of the estimator and the true parameter value is called the bias.Example: The standard sample variance formula divided by n (instead of n-1) systematically underestimates the true population variance, making it biased.Main Types of EstimatorsEstimators are classified based on how they are constructed or the properties they hold.By Construction ApproachPoint Estimators: Produce a single numerical value as the best guess for the parameter.Example: "The estimated average weight is 72 kg."Interval Estimators: Provide a range of values within which the true parameter is expected to fall, along with a specific confidence level.Example: A 95% Confidence Interval stating, "The average weight is between 68 kg and 76 kg."By Estimation MethodMaximum Likelihood Estimators (MLE): Select the parameter values that maximize the probability (likelihood) of observing the collected sample data. MLEs are widely used because they become highly efficient as sample sizes grow.Method of Moments (MoM) Estimators: Calculate the sample moments (like sample mean or sample variance) and equate them to the theoretical population moments to solve for the unknown parameters.Bayesian Estimators: Blend prior knowledge or beliefs about a parameter with the actual sample data to calculate a posterior probability distribution. The Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimate is a common point estimate derived from this method.Least Squares Estimators (LSE): Commonly used in regression, these estimators minimize the sum of the squared differences (residuals) between the observed data points and the values predicted by a model.Desirable Properties of EstimatorsWhen evaluating these different types of estimators, statisticians look for specific traits:Consistency: As the sample size increases, the estimate gets closer and closer to the true population parameter.Efficiency: Among all unbiased estimators, the efficient estimator is the one with the smallest variance (meaning its predictions are the least spread out).Would you like to see a step-by-step mathematical example of why dividing variance by n-1 makes it unbiased (Bessel's correction), or explore how Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) works?esitmation fo weights of a nerual network model using gradient descent is what kind of estimationThe estimation of weights in a neural network model using gradient descent is primarily an application of Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) or Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimation, implemented through a point estimation framework.While gradient descent is the optimization algorithm used to find the weights, the underlying statistical framework depends on the loss function you choose:1. The sample mean is an unbiased estimator of the population mean because the average of all possible sample means equals the true population mean over repeated random sampling.An estimator is unbiased when its expected value equals the true population parameter.Why It Works MathematicallyDefinition of Sample Mean: If you take a random sample of size \(n\), represented as \(X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n\), the sample mean (\(\={X}\)) is their sum divided by \(n\).Expected Value of Each Observation: Because each individual data point comes from the same population, its expected value is equal to the population mean (\(\mu \)). That means \(E(X_i) = \mu\) for every data point.Linearity of Expectation: When you take the expected value of the sample mean, \(E(\bar{X}) = E\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum X_i\right)\), you can pull out the constant \(\frac{1}{n}\) and sum the individual expectations.Cancellation: This gives \(\frac{1}{n} (n\mu)\), which simplifies directly to \(\mu \).For a detailed walkthrough of this concept, you can check out the guide on Sample mean is an unbiased estimator of population mean.Key TakeawayUnbiasedness does not mean a single sample mean will always match the population mean. There are two ways to do this:1. Sampling With Replacement (Values Can Repeat)In this method, after you select a data point, you log its value and put it back into the population before drawing the next one.Can values repeat? Yes. Because the pool of choices never changes, the exact same individual (or value) can be picked multiple times.Math impact: Each draw is completely independent, and the probabilities remain identical for every selection.2. Sampling Without Replacement (Values Cannot Repeat)In this method, once a data point is selected, it is removed from the pool and cannot be chosen again in that same sample.Can values repeat? No, the exact same individual cannot be picked twice. However, if two different individuals in the population happen to share the exact same value (e.g., two different people who both happen to be 25 years old), that value can still appear more than once in your sample.Math impact: The draws are dependent because the pool shrinks, but the sample mean remains unbiased in both methods.What "Repeatedly Chosen" Means for UnbiasednessWhen we say the sample mean is unbiased over "repeated random sampling," we don't just mean drawing values within a single sample. We mean repeating the entire experiment.If you take a completely new random sample tomorrow, and another one the day after, the average of all those different sample means will eventually equal the true population mean.Would you like to see a quick numerical example showing how the math works out perfectly when sampling with or without replacement, or are you interested in how sample size affects the chances of getting repeat values?
